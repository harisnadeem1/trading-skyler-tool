# Adds, Trims, and Maximum Committed Risk

## Why this matters

An add must come from a new, valid structure, and a trim should respond to extension or deterioration. Both change the amount of capital committed.

## Clear explanation

Once you are in the trade, manage the chart—not your fear of losing profit and not your need to be right.

Scaling can improve a strong trend, but it can also hide excessive risk.

### Valid add conditions

An add should be treated as a new decision. Require:

- open profit or reduced initial risk
- a new tight structure
- a higher low or fresh pivot
- continued market and group strength
- total position value within limits
- recalculated maximum committed risk

### Do not add because price is moving fast

Momentum alone is not structure. An emotional add far above support can turn a strong trade into a vulnerable position.

### Trim logic

Trim when the position becomes extended, concentrated, or technically climactic. Record the reason:

- extension
- resistance
- portfolio concentration
- rule-based R reference
- distribution

### Two R calculations

Track:

- result divided by initial planned risk
- result divided by maximum committed risk

This reveals whether adds genuinely improved the trade or merely increased exposure.

### How to apply it in real time

An add should not be an emotional response to profit or loss. It should occur only after the stock creates a new valid structure: another tight base, a controlled pullback, or a compact continuation pivot. Adding to a declining position without a new setup increases exposure to a failing idea.

Every add changes average price, position value, and maximum committed risk. The journal should preserve both R on initial planned risk and R on maximum committed risk. Otherwise, a trade with large later adds can appear more efficient than it actually was.

Trims can reduce exposure when the stock becomes vertical, extended from support, or enters a known resistance area. A 5R level may serve as a reference, but it is not an automatic sell signal. The quality of the move determines whether a partial trim is justified.

Moving the stop to breakeven should also require new structure, not fear of giving back profit.

## Real example or short trading story

You begin with planned risk of $400 and later add after new confirmation. Maximum committed risk reaches $650. The trade eventually earns $1,950.

That result is 4.88R on the original risk but 3.00R on maximum committed risk.

You record both numbers. Adds and trims can improve a campaign, but they must not hide how much capital was actually exposed.

## Key rules / steps

1. Require a new qualified structure before every add.
2. Recalculate total position value and maximum loss before executing the add.
3. Keep total allocation and portfolio heat within rules.
4. Use trims for extension, concentration, or structural reasons—not boredom.
5. Preserve a core position while the larger trend remains healthy when appropriate.
6. Record initial-risk R and maximum-committed-risk R separately.

**Rule to remember**

> **Track maximum committed risk, not only the risk of the first entry.**

## Common mistakes

- Managing the unrealized P&L instead of managing the chart
- Averaging down because the stock is cheaper
- Adding after vertical expansion with no new support
- Trimming automatically at a round R multiple regardless of structure
- Moving the stop to breakeven before the chart supports it

### Coach's note

Watch for **greed, fear, and the disposition effect**. You may sell winners too early and hold failures too long. Structure must determine management, not the desire to feel safe or avoid regret.

## Practice / Reflection question

### Practice

Reconstruct ten trades with adds or trims. Calculate the equity curve of the position, maximum committed risk, R on initial risk, and R on maximum risk. Then classify every add and trim as structure-based or emotion-based.

Remove any management rule that cannot be stated before the event.

Review every historical add. Mark whether a new structure existed. Compare performance of structural adds with momentum-chasing adds.

### Reflection

1. What chart behavior—not profit or fear—would change your management decision?
2. Which trailing or trimming method was chosen before the trade became emotional?
3. How will you distinguish a normal pullback from real structural failure?
