# Structural Stop Placement

## Why this matters

A structural stop belongs below the level that proves the trade idea wrong: the pullback low, base low, key level, or confirmed higher low.

## Clear explanation

The chart defines invalidation. Your account rules determine position size. Never reverse that order to force a larger trade.

A stop is the price level that proves the original trade thesis is no longer valid under normal conditions.

### Flat-base stops

Possible locations:

- below the breakout-day low
- below the final tight-contraction low
- below the pivot after a successful retest
- below the base support area

### Pullback stops

Possible locations:

- below the pullback low
- below the 30-minute higher low
- below the reclaimed key level
- below the structural support zone

### Moving averages and structure

A moving average alone is rarely enough. Combine it with an actual price level. The stop belongs below the area where the setup loses its logic.

### Do not widen

Once the trade is open, never move the initial stop farther away simply to avoid taking a loss. Widening changes a planned business expense into uncontrolled risk.

### Gap risk

A stop does not guarantee the exact exit price. Overnight news can create slippage. Position size and total portfolio exposure must account for this possibility.

### How to apply it in real time

A structural stop identifies the price action that disproves the trade thesis. In a flat base, that may be below the breakout-day low, a final tight-range low, or the base support that must hold. In a controlled pullback, it may be below the pullback low, higher low, or key-level rejection.

The exact location depends on the setup.

The stop should be planned before entry. After the position is open, emotional attachment makes objective placement harder. A stop is not moved lower because you want more time; new information can justify reducing risk, but it does not justify expanding the original loss without a new plan.

Overnight gaps and slippage mean a stop cannot guarantee the exact loss. This is why position sizing and portfolio exposure remain necessary. The stop controls normal invalidation, not every possible event.

## Real example or short trading story

You want a larger position, so a tighter stop looks attractive. But the tighter level sits inside normal noise and does not invalidate the setup.

That stop is mathematically convenient and structurally wrong.

You place the stop where the thesis fails—below the relevant low, base boundary, or support structure—then let the position-size formula adjust the shares.

## Key rules / steps

1. Write the thesis in one sentence and identify what price behavior makes it false.
2. Place the stop beyond that structural point, allowing for normal noise.
3. Check the distance against daily ATR and position-size constraints.
4. Use a hard or mental stop only according to a tested, enforceable process.
5. Never widen the initial stop to avoid admitting the setup failed.
6. Record planned and actual exit if slippage occurs.

**Rule to remember**

> **The chart decides where the trade is wrong; your desired share size does not.**

## Common mistakes

- Choosing the stop from a desired position size instead of invalidation
- Putting the stop at a round number with no structural meaning
- Using the same percentage for every stock
- Placing the stop directly on an obvious level rather than beyond invalidation
- Moving the stop lower after price approaches it

### Coach's note

Watch for **loss aversion and recency bias**. Recent losses can make valid trades feel dangerous, while recent wins can create overconfidence. Size comes from rules, not recent emotion.

## Practice / Reflection question

### Practice

On fifty historical setups, mark the earliest structural invalidation without looking at the outcome. Compare it with a fixed 3%, 5%, and 8% stop. Record which method better preserves valid trades while exiting actual failures.

Do not optimize to individual winners; study the pattern across the sample.

### Reflection

1. Where does the chart prove the thesis wrong?
2. How does that structural stop determine the position size?
3. What emotional pressure could tempt you to widen the stop, increase risk, or recover losses faster?
