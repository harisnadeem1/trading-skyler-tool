# Portfolio Heat and Correlation

## Why this matters

Several technology stocks can behave like one large position. Portfolio heat measures the combined damage if correlated positions fail together.

## Clear explanation

The chart defines invalidation. Your account rules determine position size. Never reverse that order to force a larger trade.

Individual trade risk is only part of the problem. Several positions in the same industry or theme can fail together.

### Portfolio heat

Portfolio heat is the sum of planned open risk across positions.

If four positions each risk 0.5%, total planned heat is 2.0%. This may be acceptable in a strong environment but excessive in a defensive one.

### Correlation

Five semiconductor stocks are not five independent ideas. They may react to the same index, earnings read-through, or industry news.

Group positions by:

- industry
- catalyst
- index sensitivity
- factor exposure
- overlapping key dates

### Regime adjustment

Reduce total heat when:

- the market is Defensive
- breakouts are failing
- volatility is high
- positions are highly correlated
- earnings or macro events are approaching

### Open profit is not free risk

Do not assume that unrealized gains make unlimited new risk acceptable. Market gaps can affect several positions simultaneously.

### Adds

When adding to a winner, calculate both:

- R based on initial planned risk
- R based on maximum committed risk

This prevents performance from being overstated after substantial adds.

### How to apply it in real time

Portfolio heat is the total planned loss if every open position reaches its stop. Correlation makes this number more dangerous when positions share the same market driver. Five semiconductor leaders may be five tickers but function like one concentrated bet on the same industry and index.

Risk must therefore be considered at trade, sector, and portfolio levels. In an Aggressive regime, more exposure may be justified because follow-through is strong. In a Defensive regime, the same individual setup risk can produce excessive combined exposure if several positions fail together.

Open profits do not automatically eliminate portfolio risk. Stops can gap, and highly correlated names can reverse simultaneously. You should know gross exposure, planned heat, sector concentration, and event overlap before adding another position.

## Real example or short trading story

You hold four semiconductor stocks, each risking 0.5%. On paper, they look like four separate positions.

In reality, they may react to the same index, industry news, and institutional flow. Total planned heat is 2%, and the correlation can make them behave like one concentrated trade.

You measure risk at the trade, sector, and portfolio levels before adding another related name.

## Key rules / steps

1. Sum planned dollar risk across all open positions.
2. Group positions by sector, industry, catalyst, and index sensitivity.
3. Identify upcoming earnings or macro events that affect several positions.
4. Adjust new-trade risk when the portfolio already contains correlated exposure.
5. Use the market regime to set an overall exposure posture.
6. Recalculate heat after adds, trims, and stop changes.

**Rule to remember**

> **Count correlated trades as shared exposure, not independent bets.**

## Common mistakes

- Choosing the stop from a desired position size instead of invalidation
- Counting ticker symbols instead of underlying drivers
- Assuming open profit makes a position risk-free
- Adding a “different” stock that belongs to the same crowded theme
- Ignoring currency and overnight gap concentration

### Coach's note

Watch for **loss aversion and recency bias**. Recent losses can make valid trades feel dangerous, while recent wins can create overconfidence. Size comes from rules, not recent emotion.

## Practice / Reflection question

### Practice

Take your last twenty simultaneous holdings and build a correlation map by industry and market driver. Recalculate historical portfolio heat as if every stop were hit on the same gap. Identify where the nominal 0.5% per trade rule still created excessive combined exposure.

### Reflection

1. Where does the chart prove the thesis wrong?
2. How does that structural stop determine the position size?
3. What emotional pressure could tempt you to widen the stop, increase risk, or recover losses faster?
