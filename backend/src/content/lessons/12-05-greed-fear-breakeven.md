# Greed, Fear, Breakeven Stops, and the Disposition Effect

## Why this matters

Open positions create a new psychological problem. You now have something to lose: unrealized profit, emotional comfort, or the belief that the trade proves good judgment.

## Clear explanation

Once you are in the trade, manage the chart—not your fear of losing profit and not your need to be right.

### The disposition effect

Many traders sell winners quickly to lock in the pleasure of being right and hold losers longer to avoid the pain of being wrong. This creates the opposite of professional management.

The Ronin framework reverses that tendency:

- failure is accepted when the thesis is invalid
- a healthy winner is allowed to work while structure remains intact

### Fear of giving back profit

Moving a stop to breakeven immediately can feel responsible, but the chart may not yet have created a new support structure. A normal pullback can remove the position before the trend begins.

Breakeven is a personal accounting number. The market does not recognize it as technical support. Move the stop only when price creates a higher low, new base, moving-average support, or another structural reason.

### Greed and refusal to trim

The opposite mistake appears when a stock becomes vertical, extended, or climactic. Greed turns “let winners run” into “never reduce anything.” A structural trim may be appropriate when extension, concentration, or distribution changes the trade.

A 5R level can be a reference. It is not an automatic command. The quality of the move and distance from support matter.

### Management questions

Before changing the position, ask:

1. What changed on the chart?
2. Is the decision responding to structure or to my P&L?
3. Would I make the same decision if I did not know my entry price?
4. Is the stock healthy, extended, or failing?
5. Does the new stop exist at a real structural level?

## Real example or short trading story

A trade reaches profit and you immediately move the stop to breakeven because you are afraid of giving anything back. The next normal pullback removes you before the trend resumes.

Another time, greed keeps you from trimming an obviously vertical extension.

Both mistakes come from managing your feelings instead of the chart. Breakeven and trims require structural or extension-based reasons.

## Key rules / steps

1. **Identify the disposition effect.** Do not sell winners too quickly and hold failures too long.
2. **Move to breakeven only when structure justifies it.** Emotional relief is not a chart reason.
3. **Trim from extension and the written plan.** Do not trim only because profit exists.
4. **Keep management consistent with the trend’s character.**

**Rule to remember**

> Manage the chart you own, not the emotion created by your entry price.

## Common mistakes

- Moving to breakeven only to remove emotional discomfort
- Refusing a justified trim because you want the full move
- Selling a healthy trend only because you have a profit

## Practice / Reflection question

### Practice

Review twenty closed trades. Hide the entry price and P&L, then mark where structure justified a trim, trail, or exit. Compare those marks with the actual decisions.

Identify every trade where breakeven, fear, or greed changed the management before the chart changed.

### Reflection

1. What chart behavior—not profit or fear—would change your management decision?
2. Which trailing or trimming method was chosen before the trade became emotional?
3. How will you distinguish a normal pullback from real structural failure?
